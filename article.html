Designing a Portfolio That Gets Replies

Articles / Designing a Portfolio That Gets Replies

Updated 2026 branding, ui-ux, article

Most portfolio sites do the same thing: a grid of pretty screenshots, a title, maybe a one-line tag. It looks fine. It also tells a visitor almost nothing about whether you're the right person for their project.

Show the decision, not just the result

A screenshot proves you can execute. It doesn't prove you can think. The projects that get replies are the ones with a paragraph or two next to the image explaining what the actual problem was — a form nobody finished, a checkout people abandoned, a site that looked fine on a laptop and broke on a phone — and what you did about it.

One project page beats one long gallery

If every case study lives on the same scrolling page, none of them get room to breathe, and a visitor skimming on their phone gives up after the second one. A short case-study page per project, linked from a lighter gallery, lets each project make its own case.

End with a number, if you have one

"Redesigned the checkout" is a task. "Redesigned the checkout; cart abandonment dropped from 68% to 41%" is a result. You won't have a number for every project, and that's fine — but when you do have one, it belongs in the first paragraph, not buried at the bottom.

Make replying easy

The last thing on any case study should be a way to start a conversation — not just a footer email address three scrolls away. A single button that goes straight to your contact form removes the one bit of friction most portfolios leave in.
